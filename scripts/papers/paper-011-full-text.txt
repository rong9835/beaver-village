This study examined local knowledge about the Eurasian beaver, a newly reintroduced and rapidly spreading species, and how local residents perceive its impact on ecosystem services. Locals were found to be knowledgeable about the legal status, biology, and behavior of beavers and their diverse impact on nature and ecosystem services. Perceptions included mostly negative impacts on provisioning services, while both negative and positive impacts on regulating and cultural services were perceived by local residents.

Local communities demonstrated extensive knowledge of beaver biology. Ninety-five percent of respondents knew beavers' protected status, and roughly 79 to 84% accurately identified when beavers reappeared locally and how far they travel from water. Regarding diet, a higher ratio of informants correctly thought that beavers were exclusively herbivores, and roughly two-thirds of respondents understood which parts of trees beavers consume. However, misconceptions also existed: in one area, locals presumed a connection between the arrival of beavers and a decline in the abundance of fish, despite this not being a well-supported ecological relationship.

Overall perception skewed negative: most respondents, about 72%, regarded beavers as harmful, and only about 5% viewed them as purely beneficial. Ambivalent perceptions were also common, with about 9% of informants thinking beavers were both harmful and useful at the same time.

Despite this general skepticism, locals recognized a number of positive impacts. Many respondents appreciated beavers' engineering skills, describing the beaver lodge as an impressively complex building, and expressed affection for the animals, noting that they have cute faces that are hard to dislike. Ecological benefits were also acknowledged by locals, including an increase in biodiversity, with more amphibians, fish, aquatic birds, and new plant species observed in areas where beavers were active.

Negative perceptions dominated regarding provisioning services. In one region, damage was mentioned by several informants concerning poplar plantations and fuelwood supplies that beavers had affected. Water management concerns also emerged frequently among respondents, with beaver dams and impoundments described as having a substantial impact in some areas, particularly regarding flood risk to nearby land.

The researchers concluded that communication between nature conservationists and local residents should reflect this complexity of perceptions, and that reciprocal learning between the two groups could help to moderate local conflicts over beaver management. They emphasize that cooperation between locals and conservationists could be built on the positive feelings that many locals already have about beavers, such as their perceived cuteness, curiosity, and mastery of building and engineering. One local informant's remark illustrates a common gap in understanding between conservation goals and public perception, questioning: "If beavers are so harmful, why are conservationists spreading them?" Overall, the study characterizes the general local attitude toward the beaver as viewing it as a "loveable nuisance" — an animal that is simultaneously appreciated and resented depending on the specific impact being considered.
