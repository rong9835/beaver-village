The tail of the North American beaver is large, scaly, and paddle-shaped. It serves dual purposes: to signal danger by slapping the surface of the water and as a location for fat storage.

Adults usually weigh from 11 to 32 kilograms, with 20 kilograms being typical. The head-and-body length ranges from 74 to 90 centimeters, with the tail adding a further 20 to 35 centimeters. The beaver features webbed hind feet, while its unwebbed front feet are smaller, have claws, and are highly dexterous.

The beaver, like all rodents, possesses continuously growing incisors. Beavers can gnaw bark off trees and continue to gnaw underwater thanks to a specialized lip closure that seals behind their front teeth.

Beavers are herbivorous generalists, consuming a mix of herbaceous and woody plants. They prefer aspen and other poplars, but also eat birch, maple, willow, and alder among various other trees. Contrary to widespread belief, beavers do not eat fish.

The purpose of the beaver dam is to create a deepwater refuge enabling the beaver to escape from predators. Lodges are constructed from sticks, twigs, rocks, and mud in lakes, streams, and tidal river deltas.

Beavers usually mate for life, forming familial colonies. Young kits typically remain with their parents for up to two years before dispersing to establish their own territory.

Beavers are widespread across the continental United States, Canada, southern Alaska, and some parts of northern Mexico, and thrive in boreal and temperate ecoregions.
