This research examined how beaver-engineered wetlands store sediment and nutrients, addressing the question of whether beaver reintroduction could help mitigate degradation in agricultural landscapes. The study surveyed 13 beaver ponds and found that together they held a total of about 101.53 tonnes of sediment, along with 15.90 tonnes of carbon and 0.91 tonnes of nitrogen stored within the pond sediments.

Beaver dam sequences function as sediment traps, largely due to the rapid decrease in water velocity that occurs when flowing water enters a pond behind a dam. This slowing of water allows suspended sediment, along with the nutrients and organic material attached to it, to settle out and accumulate on the pond bottom rather than continuing to travel downstream.

Larger ponds demonstrated greater sediment storage per unit area than smaller ponds. Upper-middle ponds located nearest to agricultural headwaters contained elevated sediment levels compared to ponds further downstream, reflecting their position closer to the main source of eroded material entering the stream network. The research indicates that over 70% of the sediment found in the ponds originated from upstream erosion sources, primarily linked to agricultural land use in the catchment.

Mean sediment depth across the surveyed ponds measured about 0.27 meters, with sediment accumulating at an estimated rate of approximately 5.4 centimeters per year. Pond sediment samples averaged about 14.74% total carbon and 0.87% total nitrogen by composition, values that significantly exceeded the carbon and nitrogen concentrations measured in upstream channel sediment outside of beaver ponds, demonstrating that beaver ponds act as concentrated nutrient and carbon sinks relative to the free-flowing stream channel.

These findings support the conclusion that beaver-engineered wetlands store substantial quantities of sediment and nutrients that would otherwise continue moving downstream, offering a potential cost-effective restoration strategy within intensively-managed European agricultural systems, where excess sediment and nutrient runoff from farmland is a significant and costly water quality problem. Reintroducing beavers, or facilitating their natural recolonization, could therefore serve as a low-cost, self-maintaining approach to reducing downstream sediment and nutrient loads compared to engineered human-built alternatives.
