This study examined the histology and ultrastructure of the esophagus in the European beaver (Castor fiber), focusing on features that appear to be adapted to seasonal changes in diet. The beaver esophagus features a distinctive protective design: the mucosa is lined with a thick stratified squamous keratinized epithelium, a structure similar in composition to the skin epidermis, which is unusual and notably robust compared to the esophageal lining of many other mammals.

The researchers analyzed esophageal tissue collected in spring, summer, and winter from 18 adult beavers captured in Poland, taking detailed measurements of the thicknesses of the whole mucosa, the keratinized layer, the epithelial cell layer, and other structural components of the esophageal wall.

A striking seasonal difference was found in the thickness of the protective keratinized layer: the keratinized layer was 2 to 3 times thicker in esophagi collected in winter than in those collected in spring and summer. This protective thickening corresponds closely to known dietary shifts in beavers across the year. In summer, beavers eat softer foods such as leaves and young sprouts, while in winter, their basic food consists of large quantities of hard, abrasive woody plant material, including bark and branches, since softer vegetation is largely unavailable during the cold season.

Interestingly, the epithelial cell layer thickness itself remained unchanged regardless of the season, even though the keratinized layer on top of it varied so much. However, immunolabeling for a marker of cell proliferation showed a higher index of epithelium proliferation in esophagi collected in winter compared to other seasons. This suggests that rather than simply building up a static thicker layer, the beaver's esophageal lining actively increases its rate of cell turnover and renewal during the winter months, compensating for the greater wear and abrasion caused by consuming harsh, woody winter food.

The study also noted a developmental observation: fetal beaver esophagi have epithelium covered with a keratinized layer, though thinner than that found in adults, indicating that this general protective adaptation is present from before birth and is at least partly under genetic or developmental programming rather than being purely a response learned or acquired only after an individual beaver begins eating solid woody food. Overall, the findings demonstrate that the beaver esophagus possesses a structurally and physiologically dynamic protective barrier that adjusts seasonally to accommodate the animal's markedly different summer and winter diets.
