Beavers function as keystone species, organisms whose removal can trigger ecosystem collapse. They are often called ecosystem engineers because they continuously create wetlands and ponds through dam and lodge construction, shaping the landscape around them.

The North American beaver has an average body length of about 2 feet, not including the tail, and typically weighs between 35 and 60 pounds, making it the largest rodent in North America. Beavers have a two-layered fur coat, with a dense insulating undercoat covered by longer, coarser guard hairs, which keeps them warm in cold water.

The beaver's tail is flat, scaly, and approximately one foot long. It is nearly hairless and is used for balance on land, as a rudder while swimming, and as a way to slap the water's surface to warn other beavers of danger. Beavers also have large webbed hind feet that help propel them through water, and hand-like front paws with five splayed toes that they use to manipulate sticks, mud, and food.

Beavers have several sensory adaptations for their semi-aquatic lifestyle. They have three eyelids, including a clear inner one that protects their eyes while working underwater. Their small ears and nose have valves that close automatically when they submerge, keeping water out. Beavers also have a highly developed sense of smell, which they rely on more than eyesight.

Beavers have 20 teeth in total, including 16 molars used for grinding food and 4 large incisors at the front. These incisors are covered in a thick layer of enamel that is colored orange or reddish-brown due to iron compounds within it. Because the incisors continuously grow throughout the beaver's life, beavers must constantly gnaw on wood to keep them worn down to a proper length; without this constant gnawing, the teeth would grow too long.

Beavers are typically found near riparian ecosystems containing aspen, birch, poplar, and willow trees, which provide both food and building material. They are mostly nocturnal and, although they live together in family colonies, individual beavers often work independently on tasks like foraging or dam repair. Beavers can hold their breath for approximately 15 minutes while working or swimming underwater.

Beavers are herbivores that prefer the bark, twigs, and inner layer of wood called cambium, as well as aquatic vegetation. They do not hibernate during winter; instead, they store branches and food underwater near their lodge so they can access it even when the surface of the pond freezes over. In the wild, beavers can live between 16 and 24 years.
