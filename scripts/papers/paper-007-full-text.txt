Bio-logging is a common method used to collect ecological data on wild animals, such as movement, activity, and habitat use, but it might also induce stress, reduce body condition, and alter behavior in the tagged animal. This is a concern because beavers are difficult to study directly due to their semi-aquatic, nocturnal lifestyle, which makes bio-logging devices valuable research tools but also raises animal welfare questions that need to be addressed with data.

Negative effects from bio-logging can be caused by capture stress and vary depending on the attachment method used. This study examined the impact of externally attached bio-logging tags on the body weight change of Eurasian beavers (Castor fiber), comparing tagged individuals against untagged control individuals over comparable time periods.

The researchers used glue-on tags attached directly to beaver fur. The tag was glued onto the lower back of the beaver, 15 centimeters above the base of the tail. Tags weighed approximately 159 grams on average, with tag weight varying according to the specific logger being used. The relative tag weight constituted 0.8% of the initial body weight on average, with a range of 0.5% to 1.4%. This fell well below standard thresholds used in other bio-logging research, where tags weighing more than roughly 1% of an animal's body weight typically begin to show measurable negative effects in other species such as birds.

The research involved 57 tagging periods, covering 20 female and 22 male beavers, compared against 32 control periods of untagged beavers. Tagged beavers averaged 18 days between capture and recapture, while untagged controls were observed over an average of 64 days between measurements.

The results showed a clear difference between the two groups: untagged beavers on average gained weight daily, while tagged beavers on average lost weight daily. Specifically, untagged beavers gained an average of 23 grams per day, while the body weight of tagged beavers declined by an average of 13.7 grams per day. Overall, tagged beavers on average lost weight daily, indicating a negative effect of tagging on body condition, even though the reduction in daily weight change was small (about 0.1% of body weight per day on average) and showed substantial variation between individuals.

Neither tag weight, number of tagging days, nor season were found to be important in explaining the amount of body weight change, suggesting that the negative effect was not simply proportional to how heavy the tag was or how long it was worn. The authors emphasize that studies investigating the effects of tagging should not only compare averages between tagged and untagged groups, but should also analyze individual-level impacts and compare tagged animals with a proper control group, since the effect of bio-logging equipment can vary considerably from one individual animal to another.
