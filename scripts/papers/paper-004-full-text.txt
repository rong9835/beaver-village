The invasion of a species into an ecosystem can cause population reduction or extinction of a related native species due to replacement competition. Two species of beaver (Castor spp.) exist in Eurasia today: the native Eurasian beaver (Castor fiber) and the invasive North American beaver (Castor canadensis).

Beavers are highly territorial, semi-aquatic rodents that live in family units commonly referred to as colonies. They defend their territories by scent marking with castoreum from the castor sacs, combined with urine, deposited at scent mounds around the edge of their territory. Castoreum is a yellowish, oily exudate produced by the castor sacs of mature beavers.

Identifying which beaver species is present in an area is important for wildlife management, especially where the invasive North American beaver threatens to displace the native Eurasian beaver. Traditional identification methods include chemical analysis of anal gland secretion and DNA testing, but both methods are costly and time consuming, and DNA testing in particular requires laboratory processing that delays management decisions.

Dogs (Canis lupus familiaris) are a possible non-invasive alternative for species discrimination since they have an extensive sense of smell and can be trained to detect subtle scent differences. This study tested whether trained dogs could discriminate between the scent of the two beaver species, using both castoreum samples collected from dead beavers and scent marks collected directly from the field.

Seven dogs were used in the initial trials. All seven dogs carried out 10 trials each and successfully discriminated between the two beaver species (indicating only the target beaver scent) using castoreum samples from dead beavers, with an average accuracy of 96.7%. Four of the dogs then received further training and carried out 27 additional testing trials each, successfully discriminating between the castoreum scent marks collected from the field for both beaver species, with an average accuracy of 97.8%.

The hypothesis that dogs can discriminate castoreum from the two beaver species was supported by these results. This study demonstrates that the composition of chemical compounds present in castoreum differs between the two beaver species. These differences are probably due to genetic factors arising through geographical isolation between the Eurasian and North American beaver lineages, which diverged long ago and have since evolved separately.

The findings suggest that scent-detection dogs could be used as a practical, non-invasive field tool for wildlife managers to distinguish between native and invasive beaver populations without needing to trap, handle, or genetically sample individual animals, which could speed up conservation and management decisions in areas where the two species' ranges might overlap or where an invasive population needs to be identified quickly.
