This study conducted field experiments to assess whether juvenile salmon and trout can successfully pass across beaver dams and beaver dam analogues (human-built structures that mimic beaver dams) during low flow conditions, in a tributary to the Klamath River in California, USA.

Fish were captured using hand seines and briefly anesthetized. A small Passive Integrated Transponder (PIT) tag was inserted into each fish's peritoneal cavity to allow tracking. The minimum taggable size was 65 millimeters, with most fish tagged being between 65 and 80 millimeters in length. Tagged fish were then monitored using temporary and permanent PIT antennas placed strategically around the beaver dam analogues.

For juvenile coho salmon, 91% of the tagged fish that were placed in the release pool below one of the dam analogues left the pool within 36 hours of being released. By the end of the 21-day experiment, 91% of the fish were eventually detected upstream in the pond above the dam. Of these, nearly half (49%) jumped directly over the dam structures, while 60% used side channel passages around the structure to move upstream.

Results for juvenile steelhead trout were more variable and generally slower than for coho salmon. In a pilot experiment, 74% of the tagged juvenile steelhead trout placed in the release pool were detected upstream of a single dam analogue within 3 days of release. In the main experiment, however, only 58% of the juvenile steelhead trout moved up into the beaver pond after release, and their upstream movement was notably slower than that of coho salmon.

The researchers found that close to half of the juvenile fish in one experiment crossed a 40-centimeter jump, equivalent to about 4 to 5 body lengths, even when a much gentler sloping cobble ramp was available as an alternative route. This suggests fish were often capable of, and even preferred, jumping directly over the dam rather than always taking the easier path around it.

The authors argue that current fish passage guidelines, which are often designed around concrete or engineered barriers, may be inappropriate when applied to natural or semi-natural structures like beaver dams, since fish appear to have behavioral and physical adaptations allowing them to negotiate these more porous, gently sloped, and jumpable obstacles differently than they would a hard vertical barrier. This study lends support to the hypothesis that because salmonids have evolved alongside beaver dams throughout their evolutionary history, they have developed behavioral and physical adaptations that allow them to cross such dams relatively successfully.

Regarding dam removal policy, the researchers conclude that unless there is clear and compelling evidence that a beaver dam or beaver dam analogue is preventing the movement of fish and that this is likely to have a population-level effect on the fish population, such structures should not be removed. They emphasize that the upstream pond habitat created by the dams supported thousands of fish in a stream reach that formerly ran completely dry during the summer months, suggesting that breaching or removing these dams for the sake of fish passage concerns alone would likely cause a net loss of valuable habitat rather than a net benefit to the fish population.
