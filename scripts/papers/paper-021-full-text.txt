The beaver's distinctive tail has a conical, muscular, hairy base; the remaining two-thirds of the appendage is flat and scaly. The tail has multiple functions: it provides support for the animal when it is upright, such as when chewing down a tree, acts as a rudder when it is swimming, and stores fat for winter.

Beavers are the second-largest living rodents, after capybaras. They have a head-body length of 80 to 120 centimeters, with a 25 to 50 centimeter tail, a shoulder height of 30 to 60 centimeters, and generally weigh 11 to 30 kilograms, but can be as heavy as 50 kilograms.

Beavers have four chisel-shaped incisors that continue to grow throughout their lives. The incisors are covered in a thick enamel that is colored orange or reddish-brown by iron compounds.

Beavers need trees and shrubs to use as building material for dams, which restrict flowing water to create a pond for them to live in, and for lodges, which act as shelters and refuges from predators and the elements.

Beavers usually live up to 10 years.

Beavers live in freshwater ecosystems such as rivers, streams, lakes and ponds. Water is the most important component of beaver habitat; they swim and dive in it, and it provides them refuge from land predators.
