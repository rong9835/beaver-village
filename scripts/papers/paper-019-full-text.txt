This research developed two predictive models to help land managers anticipate where Eurasian beavers will forage and where they will build dams throughout river catchments in Great Britain, as the species continues to expand its range following reintroduction efforts. Land managers need these predictions because beaver damming creates both opportunities and conflicts depending on location: beaver dams bring multiple benefits in some contexts and require active management in others. Such understanding is considered vital to ensure that the benefits beavers offer can be maximised, while minimising negative impacts on land and infrastructure in areas where dam-building would cause problems.

The first model, called the Beaver Forage Index, identifies suitable foraging habitat by combining multiple vegetation datasets using nationally available data, combining information about woodland types, grasslands, and linear woody features along waterways. The model assigns suitability values to different areas and prioritises the most reliable data source for a given land use type. Field validation showed that stream reaches with the highest forage suitability scores were about 31 times more likely to actually be used by beavers for foraging than reaches with the lowest scores, demonstrating strong predictive accuracy.

The second model, called the Beaver Dam Capacity model, uses a fuzzy inference system to evaluate the suitability of stream reaches for dam construction. It incorporates variables such as stream power at both low and high flow conditions, channel gradient, channel width, and surrounding vegetation availability. Field validation found that active beaver reaches classified in the highest, "Pervasive," dam capacity category were about 79 times more likely to be used for damming than the lowest capacity category overall, and specifically 3.54 times more likely to be dammed compared to active reaches classified in the "Rare" capacity category.

Across the catchments studied, predicted dam densities averaged between 0.4 and 1.6 dams per kilometer, though local densities in the most suitable stretches could reach up to 30 dams per kilometer. The research found that 74.1% of dams and 67.2% of dammed river reaches observed in the field fell within areas the models classified as having pervasive or frequent damming capacity, further supporting the models' practical accuracy.

Importantly for management planning, the models also revealed that in two of the major catchments studied, 64% and 56% of the stream reaches where beavers were actively present were classified as unlikely to be dammed by beavers at all, suggesting that a majority of beaver activity in these catchments involves foraging and general presence rather than dam construction. This distinction matters for management, since simply knowing beavers are present in an area does not necessarily mean dam-related conflicts will follow. These predictive models enable land managers and conservation planners to anticipate landscape changes before population expansion occurs in new regions, allowing them to plan mitigation strategies proactively in the specific locations most likely to see genuine dam-related conflicts, rather than reacting only after problems have already developed.
