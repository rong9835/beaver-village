This study examined the diving behavior of free-living, semi-aquatic Eurasian beavers (Castor fiber) using accelerometers and pressure sensors attached to wild individuals. The researchers deployed tags consisting of a small VHF transmitter and a data logger that recorded pressure and tri-axial acceleration, glued onto the animals to track their movements without requiring recapture.

The study revealed that beaver diving behavior is predominantly shallow and brief. The majority of dives were shallow, less than 1 meter deep, and of short duration, less than 30 seconds, suggesting that the majority of dives were aerobic rather than relying on prolonged breath-holding. More specifically, the median dive duration was 23 seconds, with an overall mean of about 29 seconds. The median maximum diving depth for all individuals was 0.77 meters, with a range between 0.30 and 3.58 meters across the individuals studied.

Diving occupied only a small fraction of the beavers' daily activity, accounting for just 2.8% of their daily activity budget on average, with beavers performing an overall mean of about 39.55 dives per night. This relatively low reliance on diving is likely because beavers do not depend on diving for food acquisition in the way that some other semi-aquatic or aquatic animals do; much of their foraging occurs on land or at the water's surface, with diving used more for transport and movement between locations underwater.

Movement costs, measured through body acceleration, varied depending on the phase of the dive. Energy expenditure, measured as vectorial dynamic body acceleration, was highest during the ascent phase of a dive, while the descent phase showed comparatively lower energy expenditure. This asymmetry likely reflects the buoyancy challenges beavers face: during descent, the animal must work against its own natural buoyancy to go down, but overall movement costs during ascent may also arise from transporting materials upward, air bubbling out of the fur during the rise, and the animal exhaling during the bottom phase of the dive.

The discussion places these findings in the context of semi-aquatic mammals more broadly. Living in two fundamentally different media, land and water, precludes these animals from being particularly well adapted for either environment compared to specialists in a single medium. The thick, air-filled fur that provides beavers with essential thermal insulation in cold water also creates a complication for diving: it makes these animals positively buoyant and thus increases the upthrust they must work against, and the energy they must expend, during submergence. The transitional, semi-aquatic lifestyle of animals like the beaver, similar in some respects to that of ancestral marine mammals early in their evolutionary transition to full aquatic life, incurs locomotor costs that are substantially higher than those of animals fully specialized for either terrestrial or aquatic locomotion. Overall, the beaver's diving profile in terms of depth and duration was found to be similar to that of other semi-aquatic freshwater diving animals, rather than resembling the deep, prolonged dives characteristic of fully aquatic marine mammals.
