Beavers are socially monogamous rodents that live in family groups, typically consisting of an adult breeding pair, kits born that year, and yearlings born the previous year. Both Eurasian and North American beavers typically disperse from their natal territory at about 2 years of age, but individuals were found to delay dispersal up to age seven when population densities are high, staying with their family group longer before setting out to establish their own territory.

This research examined whether territory size, resource availability, population density, and dispersal age could explain the duration of territory occupancy, meaning how long an individual beaver held onto a territory once it established one, which ranged from one to eleven years across the study population.

Young beavers that stayed longer in their natal family before dispersing, termed delayed dispersers, showed measurable advantages compared to those that left early. Delayed dispersers had a significantly greater body mass than normal dispersers at the time they finally left their family group, being on average about 31% heavier. This greater body mass appears to provide a competitive advantage when the individual eventually competes for and establishes its own territory.

The study found that duration of territory occupancy increased with older dispersal age: beavers that delayed dispersal and established in medium-sized territories occupied their territory longer than normal dispersers who left the family group earlier. Medium-sized territories proved optimal overall, balancing having sufficient resources against the energetic costs of patrolling and defending a larger area. Large territories required excessive energy for defense, while small territories lacked adequate resources to sustain the family long-term.

The maturation hypothesis offers an explanation for why delayed dispersal can be advantageous: an animal should await its physical and behavioral maturation before attempting to acquire and defend a territory of its own, since a territory acquired too early, by an individual that is not yet physically mature, may be harder to defend and hold onto.

Remaining in the family group longer also provides valuable experience. By remaining longer in the natal family group, an individual might gain parenting experience by helping care for younger siblings, and gain experience in patrolling and defending the territory before having to do so on its own. Subordinate (non-breeding) family members have also been observed provisioning kits with food before the kits emerge from the lodge, and occasionally participating in patrolling and territory defense, as well as gaining experience in lodge building and food caching skills that will be useful once they disperse and establish their own territory.

Annual reproductive success was found to decrease with the age of the territory holder, but overall reproductive success increased with longer territory occupancy duration, suggesting that the benefits of holding a territory for a longer time can offset the effects of reproductive senescence (age-related decline) in individual beavers. Overall, the findings demonstrate that dispersal decisions and territory selection have important consequences for the lifetime fitness and reproductive success of an individual beaver, with lifetime reproductive success in the study ranging from zero to six offspring.
