The study examined dentition anomalies and pathologies in wildlife as indicators of population health status. Researchers analyzed 970 beaver skulls from 1,121 individuals removed during an eradication program in Tierra del Fuego (2016-2018). Findings included hypodontia and hyperdontia occurring in 0.41% of cases each, along with artifactual tooth loss (0.30%), acquired tooth loss (0.61%), fractured teeth (0.41%), and caries (0.31%). A cranial abnormality characterized by buccal curvature was present in 0.93% of skulls, with malocclusion cases in 0.51%. These conditions appeared across all age classes with a female bias. The low prevalence of dental anomalies and associated conditions suggests minimal impact on beaver lifespan, establishing a baseline for understanding such conditions in invasive mammal populations.

Dentition analysis in mammals through assessment of tooth structure, growth patterns, and replacement has enabled sex identification, age estimation, and life history reconstruction. Dental disease prevalence, including caries from carbohydrate-based diets, fractured teeth from physical trauma, and dentition anomalies such as tooth agenesis and tooth duplication, has gained significance in ecological research.

Dentition anomalies encompass five classifications: modified tooth morphology, presence of evolutionarily suppressed teeth, tooth agenesis of normally present teeth, tooth duplication, and changes in tooth position or rotation. Understanding anomaly prevalence in wild populations under various environmental pressures provides insight into population health alongside other indicators like body condition and parasitic load.

Hypodontia (developmental failure of normally present teeth) and hyperdontia (tooth duplication) represent deviations from typical dental formulas. These non-sex-specific anomalies have associations with odontogenic capacity, limited gene flow, congenital factors, and environmental or nutritional influences, though determining precise causes remains challenging.

The beaver (Castor canadensis) is North America's largest semi-aquatic rodent. Its dentition includes monophyodont incisors (except diphyodont premolars), hypselodont incisor teeth, and hypsodont molariform teeth with intricate lophodont structure. Introduced to Tierra del Fuego in 1946, beavers progressively invaded and impacted local ecosystems. From 2016-2018, the provincial government eradicated beavers across seven watersheds to assess local-scale eradication feasibility, removing 1,121 individuals and collecting heads for analysis.

The research objectives were to assess dentition anomaly prevalence (hypodontia and hyperdontia), document tooth absence through trauma or caries, and quantify fractured teeth, caries, and cranial abnormalities in eradicated beaver populations.

From 970 analyzed beaver skulls, hypodontia occurred in four individuals while hyperdontia was recorded in four individuals. One beaver exhibiting hyperdontia also presented acquired tooth loss. Three beavers showed artifactual tooth loss; six presented acquired tooth loss. Fractured teeth by physical trauma appeared in four beavers; three showed caries. Nine skulls presented cranial abnormality characterized by buccal curvature (deformed rostrum). Five beavers exhibited improper tooth wear classified as malocclusion, with one case related to fractured teeth (20%), two to caries (40%), and two cases unexplained (40%). Overall, anomalies and pathologies showed no significant difference between sexes or age classes, though certain sex-based trends emerged.

Dentition anomalies appeared in five of seven areas, with none recorded in the Steppe region. Tooth loss conditions were documented from forested areas. Steppe beavers showed no fractured teeth or caries. Buccal curvature appeared in both Steppe and forested Mountain environments. Malocclusion incidence occurred at both Steppe (20%) and forested Mountain areas (80%).

Upper dentition quadrants showed lowest anomaly incidence (12.5%), featuring only hypodontia. Hypodontia occurred primarily in the left lower quadrant (75%), with the third molar being the most commonly absent tooth (75%). Artifactual and acquired tooth loss mainly affected the left upper quadrant. Over half of artifactual tooth losses (66.6%) involved premolar teeth, while acquired losses related to molars, predominantly the third molar. The third molar fractured most frequently (75%), followed by premolars (25%). Caries occurred mainly in the left upper quadrant.

Hyperdontia skewed toward females, while hypodontia showed a male bias. Half of hyperdontia cases occurred in females, appearing most commonly in the right lower quadrant (75%), presenting additional premolar or molar teeth. Artifactual and acquired tooth loss predominantly affected females. Females showed higher fractured teeth incidence and similar bias for caries. Buccal curvature and malocclusion mainly affected females.

Adult beavers (3+ years) accounted for substantial anomaly cases compared to younger age classes. Artifactual and acquired tooth loss occurred in 89% of adult animals. Most fractured teeth and caries appeared in adults. Fifty-five percent of buccal curvatures occurred in young beavers, predominantly with leftward curvature. Adult beavers showed highest malocclusion incidence (60%).

The study found low prevalence of hypodontia, hyperdontia, tooth loss, fractured teeth, and caries in eradicated areas. These anomalies mainly affected lower dentition quadrants. Results indicate randomness relative to sex and age, though trends emerged. Female beavers manifested predominantly hyperdontia. Findings suggest these conditions would not compromise invasive beaver lifespan. This represents the only study with age and sex-specific data from a complete population census.

Proportions of hypodontia (0.41%) and hyperdontia (0.41%) were lower than reported in smaller North American beaver samples in earlier studies. Despite congenital origins, these anomalies causing typical pattern deviations aren't considered dysfunctional tooth degradation.

Dentition anomalies, tooth absence through trauma or caries, fractured teeth, and caries occurred exclusively in forested Mountain and Ecotone regions. Forest environments provide some requirements for dentition health, including gnawing substrate and tooth wear opportunities. The Steppe's absence of fractured teeth and caries proved notable, since the predominant grasses and shrubs comprising beaver diet there may protect against pathologies compared with forested area dental health.

Beavers develop dental anomalies predominantly in lower quadrants. Invasive beavers showed this pattern, though notably hyperdontia and acquired tooth loss were recorded in upper quadrants of one male, unprecedented in previous beaver research. Terminal dental formula teeth tend toward highest absence frequency due to vestigial nature. Hypodontia in beavers revealed third molar teeth as most frequently absent.

Artifactual tooth loss, acquired tooth loss, fractured teeth, and caries incidence in invasive beavers remained low, occurring mostly in lower quadrants. Wild animals typically show caries incidence lower than captive individuals. Tooth anomalies potentially cause starvation and mortality in Castor fiber up to 0.9% of individuals in some studies.

Background indicates male beavers manifest more dental anomalies than females in some prior studies. Though anomalies and pathologies showed no significant sex prevalence in this study, hyperdontia ratios skewed female while hypodontia occurred mainly in males.

Contrary to expectations, anomalies, tooth absence, fractured teeth, caries, and cranial abnormalities appeared in both younger and older beavers. Fractured teeth and caries affected all age classes, predominantly adults over three years old. These anomalies appear non-detrimental to beaver survival, a pattern also proposed for other mammalian orders.

Anecdotal malocclusion cases in beavers involved incisor teeth in both species. Invasive beaver malocclusion likely related to fractured teeth and caries, denoted by improper wear affecting incisors, premolars, and first and second molars predominantly in right upper quadrants. Buccal curvature, a previously unreported beaver cranial abnormality, associated with trauma in medium-sized rodents.

Documented dentition anomalies and pathologies occurred in Fuegian forest environments, notably including upper quadrant anomalies, a significant finding. These invaded forest environments may function as selective pressures driving adaptive dental changes within short timeframes. Despite multiple dentition conditions in invasive beavers, low incidence suggests negligible population health effects. Buccal cranial curvature, reported for the first time in the species, shows higher young beaver incidence potentially representing new early-life mortality factors. Fractured teeth and caries predispose toward incisor malocclusion; inadequate premolar and molar wear associates with aging.
