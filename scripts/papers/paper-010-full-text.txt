Beavers are long-lived animals, capable of reaching up to 20 years of age in the wild, though in this particular long-term study population the mean age at death for dominant breeding adults was about 7 years. The study tracked individual beavers over 15 years of observation to examine how annual and lifetime access to food resources affect individual age-related changes in reproduction and body condition.

Contrary to typical senescence patterns seen in many mammals, researchers found no evidence for somatic (bodily) senescence in beavers. Instead, once fully grown, body weight increased continuously with age, even in older individuals, rather than declining as is often seen in aging animals. Adult body weight increased with age across both sexes, controlling for body length and season, although the rate of this increase gradually slowed at older ages.

Reproduction, however, followed a different pattern than body condition. Females showed an age-related decline in annual maternal reproductive output after reaching a peak at age five to six years. Peak reproductive success occurred at a minimum age of five, when mean age-specific reproductive success was 0.75 offspring per year. Litter size itself was found to be unrelated to maternal age, with the mean litter size across the study population being 1.66 kits, and litters ranging typically from one to five kits.

The most significant finding of the study concerned how local resource availability, measured through breeding territory quality as an indicator of resource history over an individual's reproductive lifetime, affected the timing of reproductive decline. Animals from lower quality territories senesced (showed reproductive decline) when younger, with individuals in the poorest territories showing reproductive decline by a minimum age of three years, while those in the highest quality territories maintained strong reproduction for considerably longer before showing any decline. Females in the lowest quality territories were significantly less likely to produce offspring at all compared to females holding higher-quality territories.

These findings support resource-dependent theories of senescence, in which the rate and timing of age-related decline are not fixed for a species but instead depend heavily on the quality of resources an individual has access to throughout its life. The results also reveal that heterochrony, meaning different timing for the aging of different biological systems within the same individual, is widespread in natural populations: in beavers, reproductive senescence and somatic (bodily) senescence appear to operate largely independently of one another rather than occurring together, since body weight kept increasing with age even as reproductive output was declining in the same individuals.
