The comeback of the Eurasian beaver (Castor fiber) throughout western and central Europe is considered a major conservation success. Beavers were anthropogenically reduced to only a few scattered relict populations in Eurasia by the beginning of the 20th century. This resulted from active human persecution, primarily hunting for fur, meat, and castoreum, which drove the species to near extinction across most of its historic range.

Traditionally, several subspecies of Eurasian beaver are recognised by morphology and mitochondrial haplotype, each linked to a relict population that survived the population collapse. Various subspecies were initially defined based on the geographic location of their refugia and subtle morphological differences between the surviving populations.

During various reintroduction programs in the 20th century, beavers from multiple source localities were released and now form viable populations across much of Europe. These programs differed in their reintroduction strategies, some using pure subspecies from a single source population and others using mixed source populations, including individuals of German, French, Scandinavian, and Russian origin.

To understand the genetic outcome of these reintroductions, researchers sampled 235 individuals and conducted genetic analysis. The research identified two major mitochondrial lineages that matched the designated subspecies and could be assigned to two major mitochondrial DNA clades, proposed to form an eastern and a western Evolutionary Significant Unit (ESU).

Notably, the findings challenged assumptions about genetic origin: except for one sample, all analysed beaver individuals in the study bear western ESU haplotypes, despite some populations (such as those with Russian origin) being introduced from source populations presumed to belong to the eastern lineage. This suggests that the actual genetic makeup of reintroduced populations does not always match the assumed subspecies origin based on release records alone.

The study found evidence of admixture between different beaver source populations in several reintroduced populations, and concluded that admixture between different beaver source populations should be generally accepted rather than avoided. This conclusion stems from observing that admixed populations show higher reproductive rates and demonstrate strong viability compared to some non-admixed populations.

The researchers argue against strict lineage preservation as a conservation goal, noting that the maintenance of purebred lineages would require extensive genetic sampling, along with strict relocation or eradication programs to remove individuals of "impure" ancestry, an approach that seems unachievable given the steadily increasing number of beavers across Europe. Instead, the study suggests that human-mediated mixing of source populations during reintroduction has, in some cases, actually benefited conservation outcomes: diversity values increase considerably when two previously isolated populations become admixed, which can improve the long-term genetic health and adaptability of the recovering beaver population.
