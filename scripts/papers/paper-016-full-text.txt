This study examined where and how wolves (Canis lupus) kill beavers (Castor canadensis), using data from GPS-collared wolves to identify 22 beaver kill sites, which the researchers then categorized into 8 categories based on habitat type.

Beavers are seasonally vulnerable to predation on land because of their own foraging and food-storage needs. From ice-out in spring through freeze-up in late fall, beavers must forage on land to increase their fat reserves and to re-supply their food caches in order to survive the upcoming winter, which requires them to leave the relative safety of water and expose themselves to terrestrial predators like wolves.

Spring creates particular vulnerability for beavers. In the study area, water levels in spring can be more than 1 meter lower than during the previous fall. As a result, many shoreline beaver lodges are left completely out of the water in spring until water levels rise again. This means beavers must travel over land, sometimes up to 100 meters, exposed to predators, in order to reach open water.

The researchers identified a three-part hunting strategy that wolves use against beavers. First, wolves wait near areas of high beaver use, such as feeding trails, until a beaver comes near the shore or comes ashore. Second, wolves use vegetation, the beaver dam itself, or other habitat features for concealment while waiting. Third, wolves either immediately attack the beaver when the opportunity arises, or ambush the beaver by cutting off its access back to the water, preventing escape.

Evidence from the study supports that wolves hunt beavers strategically rather than opportunistically: of all wolf resting clusters located less than 15 meters from active beaver habitat features, 63% were bed sites, suggesting wolves deliberately position themselves near beaver activity areas and wait for an opportunity rather than simply encountering beavers by chance while traveling.

Seasonal variation in kill site location was also observed. Of the kills that occurred in spring, 58% occurred at sites below dams and on shorelines, consistent with beavers being exposed while lodges sit above lowered spring water levels. Of the kills that occurred in fall, 80% occurred near feeding trails and canals, consistent with beavers being vulnerable while traveling overland routes to forage and gather material for their winter food caches.

Regarding predation method, in 27% of kill sites there was evidence that wolves attacked beavers while the beaver was in the water and then pulled the carcass ashore to consume it, showing that wolves are capable of attacking beavers even partially in their aquatic refuge, not just purely on land. Wolves spent an average of about 15.4 hours at kill sites, and carcass utilization, meaning how much of the beaver carcass was consumed, averaged about 98%, indicating that beaver kills represent a substantial and thoroughly used food resource for wolves when they succeed in catching one.
