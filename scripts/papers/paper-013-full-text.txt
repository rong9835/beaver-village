This study developed a model to identify where beavers could establish habitats, using remotely sensed data, in order to inform restoration planning and climate change adaptation strategies. The researchers analyzed the Snohomish River Basin in the Pacific Northwest of the United States and found that 33% of streams had moderate or high intrinsic potential for beaver habitat, while beavers were actually absent from nearly three quarters of these potentially suitable sites, indicating significant untapped restoration opportunity.

Beavers help address climate change impacts through their dam-building activities. By damming streams, beavers create pond and wetland complexes that increase spatial heterogeneity and geomorphic complexity, species and habitat diversity, and therefore ecosystem resilience to climate-induced environmental change. This matters particularly in regions like the Pacific Northwest, which faces climate challenges including reduced snowpack and altered streamflow patterns, changes that beaver dams could help mitigate through water storage and geomorphic modification of the landscape.

The mechanisms behind these benefits include the way beaver impoundments slow stream velocity, allowing sediment suspended in the water column to settle, which helps aggrade (raise the bed of) incised stream systems and reconnect streams with their floodplains. Additionally, the increase in surface water created by beaver ponds promotes groundwater recharge, storage, and supplementation of stream base flows during dry periods, functioning similarly to a natural reservoir system distributed throughout a watershed.

Rather than using traditional habitat suitability models, the researchers developed a beaver intrinsic potential model using three key variables: stream gradient, stream width, and valley width. The model assigns ranked values from 0 to 4 to each of these variables, commensurate with their level of intrinsic potential for supporting beaver activity. A final intrinsic potential score for each stream segment was calculated by summing the ranked scores of stream slope, stream width, and valley width. The resulting model classifies streams into four categories: no potential, low, moderate, and high intrinsic potential.

Field validation was conducted across 352 stream segments and demonstrated model accuracy exceeding 85%. Notably, no site that was classified as having low intrinsic potential showed any sign of past or present beaver occupancy, which strongly validates the model's predictive accuracy. Meanwhile, 59% of all sites classified as having moderate or high intrinsic potential had signs of current or past beaver occupancy.

However, land-use patterns pose real challenges for restoration planning: 59% of the sites with suitable geomorphic conditions for beaver habitat were located in human-dominated landscapes, such as industrial, agricultural, residential, and privately held land use types, which limits restoration feasibility in many otherwise suitable areas despite the underlying geomorphology being appropriate for beaver activity.

The study established that intrinsic potential models like this one offer advantages over traditional habitat suitability approaches, particularly for identifying restoration opportunities in areas where beaver population levels are currently well below the landscape's carrying capacity. This kind of modeling approach can help land managers and restoration planners prioritize where beaver reintroduction or beaver-assisted restoration efforts are likely to succeed, based on the underlying stream geomorphology rather than solely on current beaver presence or absence.
