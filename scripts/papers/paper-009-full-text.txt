Animals engage in agonistic (aggressive/conflict) behaviors for different reasons, for example, to gain access to food, territories, or mating partners. Intraspecific agonistic behaviors, meaning aggression between members of the same species, are often the consequence of territory defense, which allows individuals to obtain exclusive access to food, mating partners, and other resources.

Both the Eurasian beaver and the North American beaver are highly territorial mammals. They are obligately monogamous and live in family groups, defending a territory against neighboring beaver families and intruders. Aggressive encounters between beavers are not rare, and beavers are frequently injured during intraspecific competition, with visible scars, particularly on the tail, accumulating with increasing age.

Researchers examined tail scars from over 1,000 beavers across two long-term studies, one on North American beavers in the USA and one on Eurasian beavers in Norway, to investigate patterns of intraspecific aggression. They measured aggression indirectly by counting these tail scars, because the scars were visible for long periods, often years, and could be examined and counted during routine captures of the animals.

Male territory owners were found to have more tail wounds than females, probably because males invest more time in territorial behaviors such as patrolling and directly confronting intruders, while females may focus more time on other aspects of family and territory maintenance.

A key finding of the study was that the number of tail scars was affected by population density in well-connected landscape types, such as large lakes and rivers, but not in more isolated areas such as ponds, where individuals generally had fewer tail scars regardless of local population density. This means that simply knowing how crowded an area is does not fully predict how aggressive the beavers there will be; the connectivity of the landscape matters just as much.

The relationship between population density and aggression also differed between the two beaver species studied. In the North American beaver population occurring in large, well-connected lakes, intraspecific aggression increased with population density, meaning more crowded conditions led to more fighting. Conversely, in the already saturated (very high density) Eurasian beaver population, intraspecific aggression was found to be in a negative relationship with population density, except at the very highest densities, meaning that in an already crowded population, further increases in density did not necessarily produce more aggression and in some cases corresponded to less.

The discussion concludes that intraspecific aggression in beavers does not only depend on population density, but to a large degree also depends on landscape composition and connectivity. In landscapes where beaver territories are more isolated from one another, such as separate ponds, opportunities and incentives for direct aggressive encounters between neighboring families are reduced compared to landscapes where territories along a large lake or river are more easily accessible to intruding beavers, allowing more frequent contact and conflict.
