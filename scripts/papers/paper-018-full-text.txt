This study used shotgun metagenomics, functional screening, and carbohydrate biochemistry to investigate how the gut microbiome of the North American beaver (Castor canadensis) efficiently converts woody plants into nutrition the beaver can use, revealing novel mechanisms of xylan oligomer degradation and synergistic biomass deconstruction among different bacterial community members.

Beavers consume bark, shoots, leaves, and other fibers from hardwood deciduous trees as a major part of their diet. This is a challenging diet nutritionally, since wood is largely composed of tough structural carbohydrates like cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin that are difficult for a mammal's own digestive enzymes to break down. Previous studies have shown that beavers are capable of digesting up to 32% of the available cellulose in the consumed hardwoods, but comparatively little was known about how the beaver microbiome utilizes the hemicellulose component of wood before this study.

Analysis of the beaver fecal microbiome found that it is dominated by two bacterial phyla. Firmicutes made up the largest share, accounting for 214 operational taxonomic units representing 58.4% of the community, and Bacteroidetes made up 93 operational taxonomic units representing 24.4%. More specifically, within the Firmicutes phylum, 200 operational taxonomic units were affiliated with the class Clostridia, representing 55.9% of the community, with 143 operational taxonomic units, or 43.6%, affiliated specifically with the family Lachnospiraceae.

The microbiome employs specialized enzymes for breaking down wood. Researchers identified 27 GH43 family genes spread across 10 subfamilies on active bacterial clones, which play a particularly important role in xylan degradation, xylan being a major component of hemicellulose in wood. The presence of these genes across different bacterial community members, each carrying an incomplete enzyme repertoire on its own, enables what the researchers describe as combinatorial or synergistic substrate conversion processes, in which different microbial species work together, each contributing partial enzymatic capabilities, to collectively achieve the full breakdown of complex lignocellulosic biomass components that no single microbe could fully degrade on its own.

These findings provide crucial insights into how specialized gut microbiomes function to allow herbivores to extract nutrition from an otherwise very difficult to digest, fiber-rich woody diet. The authors emphasize that understanding these microbial mechanisms offers new insight into sequential biomass deconstruction of wood-based fibers that may be transferrable beyond biology, potentially informing industrial processes that also aim to break down woody plant material, such as biofuel production or other applications requiring efficient breakdown of lignocellulosic biomass.
