Beaver lodges are hollow stick structures with two underwater entrances. The interior of a lodge is warm and dry. Beavers live, eat, and raise their young inside these structures, which protect them from predators and harsh weather.

One beaver family can build multiple lodges within a single territory, and family groups work together to maintain and repair their lodges and dams over time.

Beavers are known for their large front teeth, flat scaly tails, and webbed hind feet, all of which help them thrive in a semi-aquatic environment. They are mostly active at night, spending daylight hours resting inside their lodges.

Beavers feed on the cambium layer of trees, which is the soft, nutrient-rich tissue found just beneath the bark, along with leaves, twigs, and other vegetation. This diet shapes much of their behavior, since finding and transporting food is closely tied to the trees and waterways where they live.

By building dams and creating ponds, beavers dramatically alter the wetlands around them, which in turn affects the plants and animals that share that habitat.

As human development has expanded, the relationship between people and beavers has grown more complex. Modern wildlife management increasingly favors non-lethal approaches to resolving conflicts, such as installing devices that regulate water levels in beaver ponds rather than removing the beavers or their dams outright.
